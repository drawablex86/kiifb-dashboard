### CHAPTER I 

### **Mandate and Approach of the Committee** 

Vide G.O.(P) No.80/2026/FIN, dated 13.07.2026, the Government of Keralam constituted a Committee to undertake a comprehensive review of the institutional, financial, governance and operational framework of the Kerala Infrastructure Investment Fund Board (KIIFB), with a view to assessing its functioning, financial sustainability and institutional effectiveness, so that an appropriate institutional framework for meeting the State’s infrastructure financing requirements in a fiscally sustainable and accountable manner can be put in place. 

##### **1.1 The Committee had the following mandate:** 

The Committee has the following Terms of Reference: 

1. To examine the objectives, mandate and role envisaged for the Kerala Infrastructure Investment Fund Board (KIIFB) under the original project prepared by the Kerala State Planning Board with the assistance of SBI Capital Markets Ltd. (SBI Caps), as approved by the Council of Ministers. 

2. To review the changes and deviations, if any, made subsequently to the original project, institutional framework and operational mandate approved by the Government, and assess their implications. 

3. To examine the resource mobilisation strategy adopted by KIIFB, including various borrowing instruments, and assess whether the borrowings were aligned with the objective of financing financially sustainable projects. 

4. To review the cost of borrowings, including borrowings through Masala Bonds and other market instruments, and assess whether the borrowing strategy resulted in avoidable financial costs or losses, including on account of parking borrowed funds in lower-yielding deposits or investments. 

5. To examine whether the mobilisation of resources by KIIFB complied with applicable Constitutional, legal, financial and audit requirements, including the propriety of assigning or diverting State revenues directly to KIIFB otherwise than through the Consolidated Fund of the State, and to identify any irregularities or systemic deficiencies, if any. 

1 

6. To examine whether the utilisation of KIIFB funds has been directed towards addressing critical infrastructure gaps, and whether the allocation of resources has been equitable across districts and sectors based on developmental priorities. 

7. To identify expenditures, transactions or financial practices of KIIFB that, in the opinion of the Committee, warrant detailed examination from the audit, financial management or governance perspective. 

8. To examine the role exercised by the Finance Department and the State Planning Board in the appraisal, approval, monitoring and oversight of KIIFB's inflows and outflows, including compliance with statutory and policy requirements such as allocations under the Scheduled Caste Sub Plan (SCSP) and Tribal Sub Plan (TSP), wherever applicable. 

9. To examine the relevance and sustainability of the continued functioning of KIIFB in the context of the Government of India's policy and regulatory framework governing offbudget borrowings by State Governments, and to recommend an appropriate future institutional framework. 

10. To review the organisational structure, governance framework and human resource policies of KIIFB, including the processes adopted for recruitment and appointment of officers and staff, identify any improprieties or systemic deficiencies, and recommend measures for restructuring the Organisation, redeployment of its institutional capacities within Government, and the future course of action regarding subsidiary or spin-off entities established by KIIFB. 

11. To consider any other matter incidental or consequential to the above, or any other issue that the Committee considers relevant for strengthening the institutional, financial and governance framework relating to KIIFB. 

##### **1.2 Approach of the Committee** 

To meet its mandate the Committee attempted a comprehensive review of KIIFB. The review covered the KIIFB's institutional structure and mandate, its legal and governance framework, resource mobilisation and borrowing, utilisation of funds, project selection and implementation, financial management, organisational structure and human resources, and the role of Government departments in its functioning. Particular emphasis was placed on examining these aspects in relation to their conformity with Constitutional and governance requirements and established fiscal principles. 

2 

In examining KIIFB's financial operations, the Committee considered the sources and costs of funds, the terms and conditions associated with borrowing, the deployment of borrowed resources and the implications of KIIFB's financial commitments for the State Government. The Committee also examined the relationship between KIIFB's financial operations and the State's budgetary and fiscal framework. 

The Committee examined the allocation and utilisation of KIIFB resources from the perspective of developmental priorities, including the distribution of expenditure across sectors and districts. It also considered the institutional processes through which projects were identified, appraised, approved and monitored. 

The review of governance and institutional arrangements covered the respective roles of KIIFB's governing bodies as well as the role of the Finance Department and the State Planning Board. The Committee also examined the organisational structure and human resource arrangements, including recruitment, appointment and deployment of personnel. 

The Committee drew upon records and information made available by the concerned Government departments, KIIFB and other relevant institutions. In accordance with the Government Order, the Committee also sourced research, analytical, technical and secretarial assistance from Government departments and external institutions and experts. 

The Committee's assessment was guided by the need to distinguish between the original objectives of KIIFB, subsequent changes in its design and actual practices. The review therefore considers the extent to which the institutional and financial arrangements that evolved over time remained consistent with the objectives for which KIIFB was originally envisaged, and the implications of such changes for the State's finances, governance and development priorities. 

##### **1.3 Scope of the Review** 

The Committee’s approach to defining the scope of the review was guided by the need to consider KIIFB not merely as a financing mechanism but as an institution operating within the wider fiscal, administrative and developmental framework of the State. The scope of the review broadly corresponds to the eleven areas specified in the Terms of Reference and can be grouped into five interrelated dimensions: 

1. Institutional structure and mandate - the original objectives and institutional design of KIIFB and subsequent changes to its mandate and framework and their implications. 

3 

2. Financial framework - resource mobilisation, borrowing instruments, cost of borrowing, deployment of funds, financial losses and the implications of KIIFB's financial commitments; 

3. Legal, Constitutional and fiscal framework - compliance with applicable legal, constitutional, financial and audit requirements and the relationship between KIIFB and the State's budgetary and fiscal framework; 

4. Developmental and governance dimensions - project selection and utilisation of funds, distribution of resources across sectors and districts, institutional oversight and the roles of the Finance Department and State Planning Board; and 

5. Organisation and future institutional framework - KIIFB's organisational structure, human resources, subsidiaries and the appropriate institutional arrangements for the future. 

##### **1.4 Organisation of the Report** 

The report is organised into six chapters, including this Chapter. The contents of the remaining five chapters are outlined below. 

Chapter II analyse the mandate of KIIFB, including its original objectives and institutional design and the subsequent changes in its mandate and framework and their implications. 

Chapter III examines KIIFB's resource mobilisation and borrowing operations, including the instruments used for raising funds, the associated costs and their compliance with Constitutional and governance provisions and accepted fiscal principles. 

Chapter IV examines KIIFB’s utilisation of funds, focusing on project selection, fund disbursement for infrastructure and land acquisition, the sectoral and spatial distribution of projects, and the share of revenue-generating projects. It also analyses departmental views on project selection and execution, compliance with statutory and policy requirements governing fund allocation, and the oversight exercised by the Finance Department and the Kerala State Planning Board. The chapter concludes with an analysis of KIIFB’s establishment expenditure. 

Chapter V examines the organisational structure and human resource arrangements of KIIFB, including recruitment, appointments, deployment of personnel and the functioning of its organisational units and associated entities. 

Chapter VI provides a summary of the findings of the Committee in the previous chapters and sets out the Committee's recommendations for restructuring, including measures relating to its borrowing, ongoing projects, liabilities, organisational arrangements and subsidiary entities.

CHAPTER II 

### **KIIFB: Initial Project and 2016 Amendment** 

This chapter covers items 1 and 2 of the Terms of Reference (ToR) of the Committee. 

1. To examine the objectives, mandate and role envisaged for the Kerala Infrastructure Investment Fund Board (KIIFB) under the original project prepared by the Kerala State Planning Board with the assistance of SBI Capital Markets Ltd (SBI Caps) as approved by the Council of Ministers. 

2. To review the changes and deviations, if any, made subsequently to the original project, institutional framework and operational mandate approved by the Government and assess their implications. 

The Chapter is organised as follows. Part one and Part two respectively addresses the items one and two of the Terms of Reference. The last part, Part three, presents the comments and observations of the Committee. 

##### **II (i) Terms of Reference - 1** 

The Kerala Infrastructure Investment Fund Board (KIIFB) first came into being in 1999 when an ordinance was promulgated on November 11, 1999. Later a bill was introduced in the Kerala Legislative Assembly and the ordinance was replaced by the Kerala Infrastructure Investment Fund Act 1999 (Act 4 of 2000). 

The objectives, mandate and role as articulated in the Statement of Objects and Reasons when the ordinance was promulgated, read, inter alia as under: 

Infrastructure development is a _sine qua non_ for economic progress. Infrastructure projects require massive capital expenditure. In the past these were met almost wholly from budgetary sources. However, the range of infrastructure projects now demanded in the State and the scale of investment that this calls for, the budget of a state can no longer accommodate the requirements. Most States have resorted to extra budgetary sources for financing infrastructure projects 

KIIFB was thus established to raise extra budgetary resources for infrastructure financing. In 1999, KIIFB raised Rs 507 crore at an interest rate of 13.25 percent. Similarly, in 2003, it raised Rs 506 crore at an interest rate of 11.00 percent. These amounts could not be used for infrastructure financing as no project was found to be financially viable. By 2012 Government of Kerala was considering closing down KIIFB. Subsequently, however, in 2014, KIIFB was sought to be restructured as a “genuine infrastructure financier.” 

5 

It was noted that while Kerala's record of investment in the social sector had brought rich dividends to the state, it lagged behind in infrastructure development. With a view to accelerating infrastructure investment a committee was set up under the chairmanship of Shri K. M. Chandrasekhar, Vice Chairman of the State Planning Board, to explore the measures to be taken to access the capital markets. Towards this end the State Planning Board appointed SBI Capital Markets Ltd to explore alternative avenues for mobilising financial resources for infrastructure development. The Concept Note prepared by the SBI Capital Markets Ltd (SBI Caps) suggested three horizons of financing. 

- (1) Immediate financing to pay for the contractors’ arrears. 

- (2) Medium term to finance the development of infrastructure projects already identified by the Government of Kerala. 

- (3) Long term requirements. Here the objective was to put in place an institutional mechanism that could be deployed to finance any future infrastructure projects without being constrained by budgetary resources. 

For long term financing of infrastructure projects, the Concept Note proposed the following: 

- (a) Recycling of capital:  When the infrastructure projects matured, free cash flow (FCF) from viable projects could be expected and it would provide an opportunity to earn a return to investors. At this stage, the government could raise funds by offloading a part of its stake. This recycling of capital would ease the pressure of funding big infrastructure projects from the books of the government. 

- (b) Alternative Investment Fund (AIF) 

- (c) Infrastructure Investment Trust (InvIT) 

- (d) Infrastructure Development Fund (IDF) 

The Concept Note proposed establishing Category I Alternative Investment Fund (AIF) under KIIFB once the portfolio of commercially viable projects had matured sufficiently to attract investors' interest. 

The Concept Note pointed out that setting up of an Infrastructure Investment Trust (InvIT) needed a sponsor and the sponsor could be any Limited Liability Partnership or Company or body corporate. On the basis of the mapping exercise conducted, the Concept Note suggested that KSIDC could be the sponsor of InvIT. 

6 

With respect to Infrastructure Development Fund, the Concept Note noted that it could be created either in the mutual fund format or NBFC format and recommended the setting up of an IDF in mutual fund format. Based on the mapping exercise conducted, the Concept Note suggested KSIDC as the sponsor of IDF Mutual Fund format. 

The Concept Note prepared by SBI Caps was examined by the Chandrasekhar Committee which submitted its report on the options to be pursued. Accordingly, Government of Kerala vide GO(P) No 583/2014/Fin. dated 29.12.2014 took the following decisions: 

- a. To introduce a Bill Discounting System for dealing with pending bills of contractors through IOUs for one year and to initiate all consequential procedures required for its introduction. 

- b. To issue General Obligations and Revenue Bonds for medium term requirements after carrying out all procedures required including amendments to the guidelines enabling Kerala Infrastructure Investment Fund Board and Kerala Road Fund Board to act as sponsors. 

- c. To initiate steps to raise funds to meet long term requirements through AIF category 1, InvIT and IDF and take consequential action to form necessary institutional framework. 

- d. To provide the seed capital required to set up these institutions. 

Government of Kerala set up an Empowered Committee of the Council of Ministers with Finance Minister as Chairperson and Ministers for Planning, Industries, Railways, Power, Ports, and Public Works as members to take all decisions relating to its implementation.  An Implementation Group headed by the Chief Secretary was also constituted. Further, the Government Order provided for administrative support from the Finance Department and also stipulated that a special unit be set up in the Finance Department to be headed by an officer of the rank of Joint Secretary. The Joint Secretary was expected to act as the Joint Fund Manager to assist Secretary Finance (Resources) who was the currently designated Fund Manager of KIIFB. A section with a Section Officer and two assistants was created under the KIIFB to provide administrative support. The Government Order also provided for equipping Kerala Road Fund Board (KRFB) and Kerala Infrastructure Investment Fund Board (KIIFB) to act as sponsors for setting up some of the fund mobilising institutions, mentioned above, for infrastructure investment. 

In the Annual Budget of 2015-16, the Finance Minister earmarked Rs 2000 crore for seed capital to be used by KIIFB to raise additional funds through new structures such as AIF, InvIT, 

7 

IDF etc. From the above, it would seem that the objective, mandate and role envisaged for the KIIFB was that it would act as a market oriented financier of infrastructure. 

##### **II (ii) Item two of the Terms of Reference** 

Major changes and deviations made subsequently to the original project, institutional framework as approved by the Government and their implications: 

In July 2016, the new Government in their revised budget speech for 2016-17 announced an anti-recession package. In the budget speech KIIFB was given a pivotal role for creating infrastructure, thereby stimulating the economy of the State through a massive hike in capital expenditure. The size of the package was Rs 20,000 crore. To finance this programme, Government proposed mobilising resources through several innovative mechanisms. Essentially, the plan envisioned raising long term funds from the capital market through Government backed agencies and special purpose entities. This, it was stated, was facilitated by the emergence of innovative institutions and changes in the regulatory framework of RBI and SEBI. Accordingly, Govt amended the KIIFB Act 1999 by an Ordinance which was subsequently replaced by the Kerala Infrastructure Investment Fund (Amendment) Act 2016. 

In order to operationalise the amended law, Government of Kerala issued executive orders vide G.O. (MS) No 315/2016/Fin dated 8/8/2016 and GO (MS) 69/2018/Fin dated 24/02/2018. The Scheme under the Act was however formally notified only in 2021. The amendment in 2016 made many far-reaching changes from the original 2014 project at several levels. 

- The institutional structure of the Board under the 2016 law was as follows: 

   - (a) Chief Minister : Chairperson (b) Minister for Finance : Vice-Chairperson (c) Vice-Chairman, State Planning Board : Member (d) Chief Secretary : Member (e) Secretary (Law) : Member (f) Secretary (Finance) : Member (g) Secretary (Finance-Resources) : Member (h) Seven independent members who are experts, who have worked in an institution of national repute in one or more of the areas of : Members Finance, Banking, Economics 

   - (i) Chief Executive Officer : Member Secretary 

The law provided that the Chairperson of the Board viz the Chief Minister, may, also invite other Ministers or Secretaries whose departments were concerned with projects under 

8 

consideration. An Executive Committee was set up under the law. This Committee was headed by the Finance Minister and comprised senior government officials, three independent Board members and the CEO. It exercised powers and functions delegated by the Board, with its decisions requiring at least two-thirds majority of members present and voting. The quorum for a meeting was to be five. The CEO of the Fund Board was the Secretary to the Committee. This was a major change in that the governance structure now provided for the highest level of governmental leadership, independent and external experts and established an Executive Committee for delegated decision making. 

A new structure called Fund Trustee and Advisory Commission (FTAC) was also constituted. The FTAC comprised not less than three but not more than five members including a chairperson and the term was two years. The members of the FTAC were to be experts with proven experience at national or international levels in any of the fields of banking, finance regulation, financial markets, administration or economics. The CEO was the Secretary to the Fund Trustee and Advisory Commission as well. The FTAC was to act as the trustee of the fund and help to ensure that all investments of the fund served the intent and purpose of the Act and that there was no diversion of funds. No such fiduciary oversight was contemplated in the 2014 project. 

- A second major deviation related to budgetary allocations for repayment obligations. Section 7 of the Amendment Act 2016 provided as under: 

_S(7) Budgetary allocation for repayment. (1)_ From time to time, Government shall make provision in the Annual Budget to defray the expenses incurred for payment of annuity or other repayment obligations and to meet operational and administrative expenses of the Board. 

The most important deviation related to the setting apart of a share of Motor Vehicle Tax as contribution to the Fund, reckoned on the basis of the Motor Vehicle Tax collected for the previous year, such share being ten percent in the first year and increased by ten percent each year up to fifty percent of the Motor Vehicle Tax in the fifth year. In addition, the Cess levied on petroleum products was also to be contributed to the Fund by the Government on the basis of the amount of Cess collected during the previous year. The transfer of funds required for the successive twelve months was to be completed in one or more instalments before the last working day of December of that year. The law did provide that the revenue realized by the Board from any project would be adjusted from the appropriation so made at the end of the 

9 

year and only the balance after defraying operational expenses needed to be provided as appropriation during the year. 

The law further provided that Government may, after due appropriation by law passed by the State Legislature from time to time make grants, advances and loans to the Fund for the purposes of this Act on terms and conditions as determined by the Government. 

Section 8 of the 1999 law was amended to vest powers in the Board to borrow and lend. This is seen to be in conformity with the original project of 2014. One important deviation is that KIIFB alone was made to act as sponsor for setting up infrastructure investment structures such as AIF, Inv IT, Mutual Funds and Infrastructure Development Fund (IDF). 

This change in resources for the Fund represented a "recurring and significant drain" on Kerala's revenue receipts<sup>1</sup> . The amount transferred to the Fund stood at Rs 19404.52 crore as on 30.06.2026 for MVT share alone. Additionally, Rs. 5038.04 crore stood transferred from Cess on petroleum products as on 30.06.2026<sup>2</sup> . Additionally, Government contributed Rs.322.07 crore. Totally, the Government transferred Rs. 24764.64 crore to KIIFB. 

- Definition of "Infrastructure" and "projects" represents another major departure from the 2014 project.  Section 2 of the Amendment Act of 2016 provides the revised   definitions. Relevant sub-sections are reproduced below: 

2 (eb) “Infrastructure” means the assets or services in any one or more of the sectors specified in the Second Schedule 

2 (f) "Infrastructure projects" include projects in the sectors specified in the Second Schedule and multipurpose projects comprising of (sic) one or more of the sectors specified therein. 

2 (ib) "project" means an Infrastructure Project and includes both physical and Social Infrastructure projects. 

Explanation to 2 (ib) states that as per this explanation "physical infrastructure projects means projects primarily intended for creation of tangible assets and “social infrastructure projects” meant intangible social assets such as schools, social services etc.

Guidelines issued in 2021 represent significant changes and deviations from the original role and structure conceptualised in 2014. 

Major changes in three areas - governance structure, fund mobilisation and expansion of jurisdiction - with regard to 23 items of infrastructure listed in Schedule 2 of the Amendment Act 2016 created a Board that was altogether a different entity. Of the three, changes in the governance structures were by way of elevating the governance structure of the KIIFB with the Chief Minister of the State chairing the KIIFB, Minister for Finance being designated as the Vice Chairperson and also the Chairman of the Executive Committee.

Additionally, it began deviating from the provisions of the Amendment Act 2016 as well. 

##### **II (iii) Implications of the Deviations** 

Deviations and changes from the role contemplated for KIIFB have impacted the State in multiple ways.  Expansion of the definition of infrastructure to include as many as 23 items appears to have impacted the remit of various Government departments. Many departments 

11 

such as education, water supply, sanitation and local infrastructure suffered. Along with certain departments, certain regions of the State also did not get their due share. 

Diversion of State revenue by way of appropriation of MV Tax and Cess on petroleum products took away a major revenue stream adversely affecting the 'flexibility’ without providing the off-budget benefits originally envisaged<sup>3</sup> . The higher cost of raising funds, inefficient liability management  and release of borrowed funds as grants has now resulted in a huge burden on the State. 

Governance issues are equally stark - the designation of CEO KIIFB as an Ex-Officio Secretary to the Government without even a concomitant amendment to the Rules of Business created an ambiguous and confusing situation. The entire set up in KIIFB appeared to be cut off from the State Government. Furthermore, the   lack of transparency in consultancy payments stuck out a mile. These implications for balanced fund allocation and regional development, fiscal management and governance are discussed in greater detail in the subsequent Chapters. 

The 2014 project’s long-term financing strategy depended on recycling capital through an AIF, InvIT or IDF once a portfolio of revenue-generating projects matured.  KIIFB’s actual borrowing has come entirely from conventional guaranteed debt - bank loans, institutional loans, bonds and the Masala Bond - with no AIF/InvIT/IDF structure ever used. This is an important deviation: KIIFB did not evolve along the path approved in 2014, it took a different one, and that different path is what produced the cost and risk findings of Chapter 3. 

KIIFB was created because “the budget of a State can no longer accommodate” infrastructure financing. The 2016 Section 7 earmarking of Motor Vehicles Tax (rising to 50 per cent) and the whole of the petroleum cess is a fixed, escalating, statutory first charge that cannot be reprioritised in a difficult year. Chapter III (para 3.4) shows State transfers now meet 99.4 per cent of KIIFB’s debt service - the mechanism designed to relieve the budget has instead created a new claim on it.

##### **Scope, structure and sources** 

This Chapter examines the resource mobilisation of KIIFB since the amendment of its Act in 2016. As noted in the previous Chapter, the amended Act permits KIIFB to receive budgetary allocations from the Government of Keralam and to borrow from banks, financial institutions and the financial markets, in addition to earning internal revenue from assisted projects. The Chapter is organised into four Parts: 

- Part I - the law under which KIIFB draws resources from the State Government, quantum of those transfers. 

- Part II - the law under which KIIFB borrows from financial markets and financial institutions, the quantum and terms of such borrowing, and the question of prior Government sanction.

#### **Part I - Resource Mobilisation from the Government of Keralam** 

##### **3.1 The statutory basis for transfers from the State** 

Section 3(2) of the KIIFB (Amendment) Act 2016, governs the transfer of funds from the State Government to the Fund. Section 3(2) reads as follows: 

3(2) There shall be credited to the fund, - 

- (a) the amounts released to the Board from the budget allocation under section 7; 

- (b) the amount borrowed by the Board under section 8; 

- (c) grants or loans or advances made by any institution; 

- (d) any amount raised by the Board from other sources to augment the resources of the Board; 

- (e) any other amount which, under the provisions of the scheme, shall be credited to the fund 

The second, and the operative provision for present purposes, is Section 7 as substituted by the Amendment Act of 2016. It is reproduced in full: 

7. Budgetary allocation for repayment. - (1) From time to time, Government shall make provision in the Annual Budget to defray the expenses incurred for payment of annuity or other repayment obligation incurred in lieu of section 8 and to meet operational and administrative expenses of the Board: 

Provided that Government shall set apart a share of the Motor Vehicle Taxes as contribution to the fund, reckoned on the basis of the Motor Vehicle Taxes collected for the previous year such share being ten per cent in the first year and increased by ten percent each year upto fifty per cent of the Motor Vehicle Tax in the fifth year: 

Provided further that in addition, the cess levied on petroleum products shall also be contributed to the Fund by the Government on the basis of the amount of cess collected during the previous year. 

14 

Three features of Section 7 deserve emphasis at the outset, because the whole of the later constitutional discussion in Part III turns on them. 

- The section title is “Budgetary allocation for repayment”. The section is, on its own terms, a provision for the State to fund the servicing of KIIFB’s borrowings. It is not framed as a provision for capital investment by the State. 

- The obligation is mandatory in form - “Government shall make provision” and “Government shall set apart a share”. The State therefore binds itself by the statute to a continuing annual charge, the size of which is determined not by the State’s fiscal capacity in a given year but by the debt KIIFB has contracted under Section 8. 

- The revenue streams earmarked - Motor Vehicle Tax and the cess on petroleum products - are State tax revenues. Their assignment to a body outside the Consolidated Fund is examined in Part III against Articles 266 and 293 of the Constitution and against paragraph 12.22 of the Sixteenth Finance Commission Report. 

- The Committee observed that the escrow mechanism appears to have served not merely as a mechanism for ensuring the immediate transfer of funds to KIIFB, but also as an indication that accounting itself was not correct. The arrangements are inconsistent with sound accounting principles and standards. A transfer of a portion of the Government’s revenue to KIIFB should have been accounted for as a grant-in-aid and, consequently, as revenue expenditure. Instead, directly debiting Major Head 5475 - Capital Outlay on Other General Economic Services presented the transfer as capital expenditure by the Government, thereby overstating the State’s capital expenditure in the Finance Accounts. 

The third provision is Section 9, which, as amended, converts the Government’s power to guarantee into a duty: 

9. Guarantee by Government of loans. - (1) The Government shall guarantee the payment of the principal and interest of any fund proposed to be raised by the Board under section 8: 

- Provided that the total of guarantee issued by the Government under this Act shall not exceed the limits set by the Kerala Ceiling on Government Guarantees Act, 2003 (30 of 2003) in force. 

The contrast with the pre-2016 position is material. Section 9(1) as originally enacted read “ _The Government may guarantee, in such manner as they think fit …”_ and carried a proviso capping the aggregate guarantee at rupees one thousand crores (raised to two thousand crores by the Amendment Act of 2002). The 2016 amendment removed both the discretion and, in substance, the statutory ceiling on guarantees under this Act. Every rupee borrowed by KIIFB since 2016 has therefore been a guaranteed borrowing as a matter of statutory obligation, not of executive judgement exercised case by case.

#### **Part II Borrowing from Financial Markets and Financial Institutions** 

##### **3.5 The statutory basis for borrowing** 

The power to borrow is conferred by Section 8. In its original form, as enacted in 1999, the section read: 

8. Power of the Board to borrow and lend. - (1) The Board may, from time to time, with the previous sanction of the Government subject to such conditions as the Government may, by general or special order determine, borrow any sum required for the purposes of this Act whether, by the issue of bonds or debentures or otherwise or by making arrangements with Banks or Institutions approved by the Government in that behalf. 

- (2) Bonds or debentures issued by the Board under this section shall be issued, transferred, dealt with or redeemed in such manner as the Government, may, by general or special order, direct. 

- (3) Subject to the provisions of this Act and such conditions and limitations as may be specified in the scheme, the Board may, out of its funds, grant loans and advances to any public sector undertakings or other undertakings for the time being in force in the State. 

Section 8 was substituted by the Amendment Act of 2016. The substituted section retains the requirement of previous Government sanction and widens the permissible instruments to include 

17 

General Obligation Bonds, Revenue Obligation Bonds, Land Bonds and other appropriate instruments, and permits the raising of funds through arrangements with banks, multilateral funding agencies or other institutions approved by the Government in that behalf. It also empowers the Board to act as sponsor of modern infrastructure financing structures: 

… (3) The Board may act as the sponsor for setting up infrastructure investment structures, as are required for facilitating the mobilisation of resources for a project … [including an] Infrastructure Development Fund (IDF). 

(4) The Financial instruments and securities issued by the Board under this section shall be issued, transferred, dealt with or redeemed in such manner as decided by the Board subject to the directions of the Government. 

The Committee draws attention to the change brought between the 1999 sub-section 8(1&2) and the 2016 sub-section 8(4). Under the original provision, the manner of issue, transfer, dealing and redemption of the Board’s paper was to be as “the Government may, by general or special order, direct” - that is, the Government had to act, by an order, before the Board could act. Under the substituted provision, the same matters are “as decided by the Board subject to the directions of the Government” - that is, the Board acts, and the Government’s role is reduced to a residual power of direction that need not be exercised at all. The initiative, in law, moved from the Government to the Board in 2016. This shift is directly relevant to the question of sanction considered at paragraph 3.12 below. 

Two further provisions inserted in 2016 complete the picture. Section 8A permits delegation: 

8A. Power of the Board to delegate - The Board may delegate to the Executive Committee or the Chief Executive Officer such powers and functions under this Act or scheme as it may consider necessary for the efficient administration of the fund, subject to such restrictions and conditions if any, as it may specify. 

And Section 6D constitutes the Fund Trustee and Advisory Commission as trustee of the Fund: 

6D. Functions of Fund Trustee and Advisory Commission.- (1) The Commission shall act as the trustee of the fund and help to ensure that all investments of the fund serve the purpose and intent of the Act and that there is no diversion …

##### **3.12 Was the prior sanction of the Government obtained, and by whom?** 

Section 8(1) permits the Board to borrow only “with the previous sanction of the Government subject to such conditions as the Government may, by general or special order determine”. This is not a formality. It is the single statutory check standing between a body corporate with no independent revenue of its own and an unlimited capacity to create liabilities that Section 9 obliges the State to guarantee and Section 7 that obliges the State to service. The Committee therefore examined three questions. 

##### **3.12.1 Was sanction accorded at all?** 

KIIFB’s position is that every borrowing was preceded by sanction. The Committee accepts that sanction orders exist for the borrowings; the totals of “amount sanctioned” and “amount availed” reported in the Appendix statement are themselves evidence that a sanctioning process was followed, and the gap between the two (Rs 6,366.24 crore undrawn as on 30 June 2026) indicates that sanction was granted for a ceiling and drawn against as required. 

##### **3.12.2 Did the sanction emanate from the Finance Department of the Government of Keralam?** 

This is the material question, and on it the record placed before the Committee is deficient. “Government” is defined in Section 2(e) of the Act as “the Government of Kerala”. A sanction under Section 8(1) is therefore a sanction by the State Government, expressed through the department to which the business is allocated under the Rules of Business - in a matter of borrowing and guarantee, the Finance Department. 

The Committee called for the sanction orders relating to the borrowings listed in the Appendix, together with the file notings recording the Finance Department’s examination of each proposal. What was made available consisted substantially of approvals recorded within KIIFB - by the Board, its Executive Committee, or the finance wing of KIIFB acting under powers delegated pursuant to Section 8A. The Committee noted that only the first three guarantees, covering KIIFB borrowings of Rs. 18,246.73 crore, were sanctioned by the PU-B Wing of the Finance Department on 19.07.2018, 17.03.2021 and 02.11.2021, respectively. However, the guarantee deeds, fixing the tenure, rate of interest and other conditions of borrowing, related to these guarantees were not submitted to the Finance Department for approval. All subsequent guarantees were issued by the Finance–Infrastructure wing of KIIFB on the basis of an approval issued by a Deputy Secretary of the Finance Department without the approval of the Finance Secretary and Finance Minister or Council of Ministers. The Committee also noted that the Finance Department had no access to the files of the Finance–Infrastructure Wing of KIIFB. 

The Committee also noticed a specific instance in which the Finance Department’s decision not to provide guarantees for KIIFB borrowings during 2023-24, 2024-25 and 2025-26 was overruled. The Finance Department had declined to provide the guarantees on the ground that KIIFB’s borrowings would reduce the State’s borrowing space. This decision was subsequently overruled on the basis of the minutes of a meeting convened by the Chief Minister with the Finance Minister, Additional Chief Secretary (Finance) and CEO, KIIFB and GO was issued on this basis by the Finance (Infrastructure) wing of KIIFB<sup>1</sup> 

1 See: G.O.(P) No.33/2025/FIN, Dated 24-03-2025. 

23 

The Committee is of the view that such overruling of the Finance department’s decision is inconsistent with the Rules of Business of the Government. Such a decision of the Finance department can be overruled only by the Council of Ministers, and the department is required to issue a Government Order in accordance with the Cabinet decision. 

The Committee therefore records that no evidence was furnished to establish that the previous sanction required by Section 8(1) of KIIFB (Amendment) Act 2016 was, in each case, accorded by the Finance Department of the Government of Keralam as distinct from an authority within KIIFB. On the contrary, Finance Department’s decisions on KIIFB’s borrowings were overruled by adopting procedures that violate the Rules of Business of the State. 

##### **3.12.3 If sanction was accorded from within KIIFB, does that satisfy the spirit of Section 8?** 

It does not, and the Committee considers the point important enough to state plainly. 

It may be said in answer that the composition of KIIFB makes the distinction artificial. The Board is chaired by the Chief Minister; the Finance Minister is a member; the Chief Secretary and the Secretaries dealing with Finance, Finance-Resources, Law are members; and the Chief Executive Officer is the Fund Manager. On that view the body that sanction is, in personnel and in authority, the same body that would sanction if the file moved to the Finance Department, and requiring a separate order is empty formalism. 

The Committee does not accept that view, for four reasons. 

- Identity of persons is not identity of capacity. An officer sitting as a member of a statutory Board exercises the Board’s powers under the Act. The same officer in the Finance Department exercises the executive power of the State under the Rules of Business, subject to different records, different concurrences and different accountability to the Legislature. Section 8(1) deliberately distinguishes “the Board” from “the Government”. 

- The proposer cannot be the approver. The entire purpose of an external sanction is to interpose a decision-maker who does not own the proposal. Where the borrowing is proposed by KIIFB’s management, appraised by KIIFB’s finance wing, and sanctioned by KIIFB’s Board or a delegatee of that Board under Section 8A, no independent mind has been applied at any stage. The safeguard becomes self-certification. This is a defect of design, and it is not cured by the good faith or seniority of the persons involved. 

- The Fund Trustee and Advisory Commission does not fill the gap. Section 6D makes the Commission the trustee of the Fund, charged with ensuring that investments serve the purpose and intent of the Act and that there is no diversion. Its function is fidelity of application, certified half-yearly. It is not charged with, and does not perform, the Finance Department’s function of assessing whether a proposed borrowing is the cheapest available, correctly timed, or consistent with the State’s own fiscal position and borrowing calendar. 

- The consequences fall outside KIIFB. Every borrowing sanctioned within KIIFB carries a mandatory State guarantee under Section 9, a mandatory State servicing obligation under Section 7, and - since 2021-22 - an automatic reduction of the State’s own Net Borrowing Ceiling under the Government of India guidelines discussed at paragraph 3.17. The body 24 

bearing these consequences is the State. A sanction regime in which the State’s Finance Department is not demonstrably the sanctioning authority leaves the party that bears the risk out of the decision that creates it. 

The Committee accordingly records that, on the material before it, the requirement of previous Government sanction under Section 8(1) of KIIFB (Amendment) Act 2016 has been observed in form and, so far as the record discloses, not in substance. 

##### **3.13 The legality of chit-based fund mobilisation** 

Money originating in chitties conducted by KSFE (Rs 1237.14 crore outstanding) is the largest source of KIIFB’s funds drawn directly from individual subscribers. Because this is subscriber money rather than institutional money, and because the subscribers are non-residents, the Committee has examined its legality separately from its quantum. 

##### **3.13.1 The chitty itself is lawful** 

Chit funds fall within Entry 7 of List III of the Seventh Schedule and are regulated by the Chit Funds Act, 1982 (Central Act 40 of 1982). Section 2(b) defines a chit as follows: 

Chit’ means a transaction whether called chit, chit fund, chitty, kuri or by any other name by or under which a person enters into an agreement with a specified number of persons that every one of them shall subscribe a certain sum of money (or a certain quantity of grain instead) by way of periodical instalments over a definite period and that each such subscriber shall, in his turn, as determined by lot or by auction or by tender or in such other manner as may be specified in the chit agreement, be entitled to the prize amount. 

KSFE is a company wholly owned by the Government of Keralam and has conducted chitties as foreman since 1969. Every chit requires the previous sanction of the Registrar of Chits under Section 4 and the filing and registration of the chit agreement. Nothing in the Act prevents a State-owned company from acting as foreman. 

##### **3.13.2 Non-resident subscription is lawful** 

Non-residents are not barred from investing in the business of chit funds on a non-repatriation basis. Two conditions attach. First, the subscription must be brought in through normal banking channels, including through an account maintained with a bank in India. Second, the Registrar of Chits, or an officer authorised by the State Government in that behalf, must permit the particular chit fund to accept subscriptions from non-residents on a non-repatriation basis. 

The Committee therefore does not doubt that the scheme is capable of being operated lawfully. 

##### **3.13.3 The real question is the onward deployment of chit moneys into KIIFB paper** 

The legality of the chitty does not settle the legality of what is done with the money collected under it. Section 14 of the Chit Funds Act restricts that use exhaustively: 

25 

14. Utilisation of funds.- (1) No person carrying on chit business shall utilise the moneys collected in respect of such business (other than commission or remuneration payable to such person or interest or penalty, if any, received from a defaulting subscriber), except for- (a) carrying on chit business; or (b) giving loans and advances to non-prized subscribers on the security of subscriptions paid by them; or (c) investing in trustee securities within the meaning of section 20 of the Indian Trusts Act, 1882 (2 of 1882); or (d) making deposits with approved banks mentioned in the chit agreement. 

The list is closed. Subscribing to Security Bonds, Deposit Bonds or Corporate Papers issued by KIIFB is plainly not the carrying on of chit business, is not a loan to a non-prized subscriber, and is not a deposit with an approved bank - KIIFB is not a bank. The deployment can therefore be lawful only if KIIFB’s instruments are “trustee securities within the meaning of section 20 of the Indian Trusts Act, 1882”. Everything turns on that single gateway. 

Section 20 of the Indian Trusts Act, as it stood before 2016, enumerated the permissible categories, and those categories included securities the interest on which is guaranteed by the Central Government or a State Government. Since Section 9 of the KIIF Act obliges the Government of Keralam to guarantee the payment of principal and interest on funds raised by the Board, a respectable argument could be made on that text that KIIFB’s instruments qualified. 

That text, however, no longer stands. The Indian Trusts (Amendment) Act, 2016 (Act 34 of 2016) substituted Section 20 in its entirety: 

20. Investment of trust-money.- Where the trust-property consists of money and cannot be applied immediately or at an early date to the purposes of the trust, the trustee shall, subject to any direction contained in the instrument of trust, invest the money in any of the securities or class of securities expressly authorised by the instrument of trust or as specified by the Central Government, by notification in the Official Gazette … 

The substituted section deletes the enumerated categories altogether. Eligibility now depends on a notification of the Central Government. KIIFB’s issuances to KSFE began on 22 November 2018, after the substituting Act received assent on 26 July 2016. Two questions therefore arise which the Committee is not in a position to answer on the material before it: 

- Which version of Section 20 of the Indian Trusts Act was in force on the dates of the successive placements? The Amendment Act of 2016 was to come into force on a date to be appointed by the Central Government by notification, and the Committee has not been shown the commencement notification or a statement of the position on each placement date. 

- If the substituted section was in force, has the Central Government notified any class of securities that would include State-guaranteed bonds of a statutory body such as KIIFB? If it is not, the gateway in clause (c) of Section 14(1) may be narrower than the parties assumed, or may not be available at all. 

26 

**3.13.4 The Committee’s conclusion on legality** 

The Committee does not find that the arrangement is unlawful. It finds something less comfortable: that its legality rests entirely on one narrow statutory gateway, that the gateway was materially altered by Parliament two years before the placements began, and that no contemporaneous legal opinion, no Registrar’s permission, and no record of any examination of Section 14 of the Chit Funds Act or of Section 20 of the Indian Trusts Act was placed before the Committee. A structure through which Rs 1,237.14 crore of ordinary subscribers’ savings has been channelled into the unsecured paper of a single State entity ought to rest on a recorded legal foundation which the Committee has not been shown.  The Secretary (Law) in discussion with the committee, expressed the view that investment of chit moneys in KIIFB bonds is within Section 20 of the Indian Trusts Act, 1882, and written advice to that extent is understood to exist. No contemporaneous legal examination was placed before the committee either by KIIFB or KSFE at the time of placements, but the Committee takes note of the Secretary’s advice. 

Separately from legality, the Committee records a question of prudence. Even on the assumption that every placement is lawful, the entire deployment of Pravasi Chitty moneys is with a single counterparty whose debt service is met as to 99.4 per cent by transfers from the State. The subscribers are protected by the State guarantee under Section 9, and the Committee does not suggest that their money is at risk. The Kerala Pravasi Chit Fund, placing the whole of its investible surplus with one related entity (KIIFB) with no counterparty diversification whatsoever and having no prudential investment policy or exposure limit, in the Committee’s view, imprudent. 

#### **PART III - CONSTITUTIONAL, LEGAL AND FISCAL ISSUES** 

##### **3.14 The constitutional framework** 

Four constitutional provisions bear on KIIFB’s resource mobilisation. 

- Article 266(1) requires that all revenues received by the Government of a State form one consolidated fund, the Consolidated Fund of the State. Article 266(3) provides that no moneys out of the Consolidated Fund shall be appropriated except in accordance with law and for the purposes and in the manner provided in the Constitution. 

- Articles 202 to 204 require the annual financial statement to be laid before the Legislature and expenditure to be authorised by an Appropriation Act. Legislative control over the public purse operates through these provisions. 

- Article 293(1) empowers a State to borrow within the territory of India upon the security of the Consolidated Fund of the State, within limits fixed by the State Legislature. Article 293(3) requires the consent of the Government of India for any such borrowing while any part of a loan from the Union remains outstanding. 

- Entry 37 of List I of the Seventh Schedule places “foreign loans” exclusively within the legislative competence of the Union. A State has no power to raise a loan from outside India. 

The questions that follow are whether the earmarking of Motor Vehicles Tax and petroleum cess to KIIFB otherwise than through the Consolidated Fund is consistent with Article 266; whether KIIFB’s borrowing, serviced from State revenues, bypasses Article 293; and whether the Masala Bond issue encroached on Entry 37 of List I.

##### **3.15 Off-budget borrowing: the position of the Comptroller and Auditor General** 

KIIFB was originally institutionalised as a borrowing mechanism outside the State budget for revenue-earning infrastructure investment. The 2016 amendment broadened the range of eligible projects to include the social sector, which by its nature generates no revenue for KIIFB. As shown at paragraph 3.20, the larger share of KIIFB’s investment has gone to non-revenue-earning projects<sup>2</sup> . That necessitated a constant flow of resources from the State budget to KIIFB to repay and service its borrowing - not merely a guarantee of that borrowing. 

The Comptroller and Auditor General of India, in the State Finances Audit Report of Keralam for the year ended 31 March 2019, held that KIIFB’s borrowings are off-budget borrowings which bypass the limits set under Article 293. The relevant passage (pages 41-42) reads: 

The borrowings of the State Government are governed by Article 293(1) of the Constitution of India, the guiding principle of which is that the State Government is free to borrow money within the territory of India upon security of the Consolidated Fund of the State, and the limits on such borrowings can be regulated under Article 293(3) of the Constitution of India. The State Government is, therefore, required to furnish to GOI the financial statements showing the estimates of receipts and repayments of all sources of borrowings including Open Market Borrowings, other liabilities arising out of Public Account transfers etc, based on which the GOI gives the consent for raising loans and fixes the borrowing ceiling for the State Government. In respect of Kerala the limit fixed for 2018-19 was 3 per cent of the estimated GSDP. 

KIIFB’s borrowings represent off-budget borrowings, which bypasses the limits set on Government borrowings under Article 293 of the Constitution of India. These borrowings are not taken into the disclosure statements in the budget documents or in the accounts. Hence such borrowings do not have legislative approval. 

In the same Report the Comptroller and Auditor General drew the distinction, central to this Chapter, between a genuine contingent liability arising from a guarantee to a public sector undertaking and a direct liability dressed up as a guarantee (pages 42-43): 

A State may have a number of PSUs, ABs etc., who may also borrow from the market; repayments of these borrowings and interest thereon are a charge on the income of the concerned PSUs/ABs. However, depending on the financial health of the PSUs/ABs, such borrowings may sometimes have to be against Government guarantees. Such guarantees are obviously in the nature of contingent liabilities. It is for this reason that the relevant Acts of most States prescribe a maximum limit or extent to which Government guarantees may be given. In the case of Kerala, the Kerala Ceiling on Government Guarantee Act, 2003, amended w.e.f. April 2018, provides a limit of five per cent of GSDP. However, even in cases where Government guarantees are given, the basic presumption is that the PSU/AB would be able to repay the borrowed sum along with interest from its own sources of revenue. That is why it is not considered fully as the State’s liabilities, but only a contingent liability, because the State would have to discharge the liability only in the contingency that the PSU/AB fails to discharge its debt obligation. It may be 

> 2 Also see paragraph 4.2.2 in Chapter 4 

28 

noted that a guarantee can be treated as contingent liability only when the borrowing entity generates its own resources and is expected to repay the borrowings from such resources; only in the contingency of the borrowing entity failing to repay does it become the liability of the State as the guarantor. The meaning of the English word ‘contingent’ in this case is very clear, and brings into focus the nature of the liability. 

As a corollary, if the PSU/AB has no sources of income and has to depend on flow of funds from the State Government to meet its debt obligation, then such liability is obviously fully the liability of the State. In other words, if the resources of the PSU/AB are to come from revenue raised by the Government by way of taxes and cesses, raised on the tax-raising authority of the State, and the PSU/AB has no income of its own, then there is nothing contingent about the liability; the liability is tied up to the revenue resources of the State and is, therefore, a direct charge on the State revenues. It is obvious that there is nothing ‘contingent’ about a liability which has to be repaid in this manner. 

The Committee has tested that reasoning against the figures. The test the Comptroller and Auditor General proposes is whether the borrowing entity generates its own resources and repays from them. Table 3.2 answers it: over 2021-22 to 2024-25, State transfers of Rs 11,869.28 crore met 99.4 per cent of KIIFB’s debt service of Rs 11,944.18 crore. ##### **3.18.1 The Masala Bond: cost and the claimed benefit** 

The Masala Bond was one of the most expensive borrowings that KIIFB raised. At 9.723 per cent against the State’s weighted average open market borrowing cost of 8.31 per cent in 2018-19, the coupon carried a premium of 141 basis points. Over the five-year tenure, on Rs 2,150 crore, this amounts to approximately Rs 152 crore of additional interest - interest actually paid of Rs 1,045.44 crore against approximately Rs 893 crore had the same sum been raised at the State’s own 2018-19 cost. To this add Rs. 29.73 crore, of other expenses of Masala bond presented in Table 3.14 Thus, the Masala Bond itself caused an excess cost of around Rs. 181.73 crore. 

33 

KIIFB has advanced in mitigation the argument that the international issue established a credit history and a pricing benchmark for a sub-sovereign issuer, which enabled it subsequently to raise domestic funds at lower cost. The Committee has examined that claim against the record and does not find it made out, for three reasons. 

- The claim is not borne out by the subsequent pricing. If the Masala Bond had created a durable pricing benefit, KIIFB’s spread over the State’s cost of funds should have narrowed after 2019. It did not. 

- KIIFB’s domestic bond coupons rose rather than fell, from 8.49 per cent in July 2023 to 9.67 per cent in August 2025. The Committee accepts that the general interest rate environment changed over this period, which is why the comparison above is made against the State’s own contemporaneous cost of funds rather than in absolute terms. On that like-for-like basis no improvement is visible. 

- No contemporaneous record was produced showing that the benchmark benefit was evaluated and quantified either before the issue was decided upon or afterwards. A claimed strategic benefit that was never costed at the time of decision cannot be relied upon after the event to justify a 141basis point premium on interest rate and other expenses. 

The Committee would add, in fairness, that the issue was a genuine first for a sub-sovereign Indian issuer, that it was made in a rupee-denominated form so that no exchange risk was assumed by KIIFB or the State, and that it was serviced and redeemed in full and on time. Those are not small things. But they do not establish that the premium paid was the cheapest way of achieving the objective. 

##### **3.18.2 Different instruments at different coupons in the same year** 

The most significant feature of Table 3.12 for audit purposes is not the spread over the State’s cost but the spread within KIIFB’s own borrowing in a single year. The spread is always higher than 100 basis points and it range from 139 basis points in 2021-22 and 2022-23 to 271 basis points in 202526. The instruments concerned were not separated by long intervals. In 2024-25, NABARD NIDA Tranche 4 was drawn at 7.25 per cent on 25 October 2024, and Domestic Bonds Series 4 were issued at 9.42 per cent on 30 December 2024 - a difference of 217 basis points within ten weeks. 

There may be sound reasons for such differences. Concessional institutional lines such as NABARD NIDA carry sectoral eligibility conditions, disbursement linked to physical progress, and ceilings that cannot be exceeded; a bond issue may be the only instrument capable of delivering a large sum on a fixed date. If those were the reasons, they should have been evidenced in the records. 

The Committee called for the appraisal notes recording why, in each year in which instruments of materially different cost were used, the more expensive instrument was preferred - that is, on a comparative evaluation of available sources with the headroom remaining on the cheaper lines, the reason the cheaper lines could not meet the requirement. No such evidence was furnished for any 

34 

year. The Committee therefore records that no evidence was placed before it to justify the preference of a higher coupon over a lower coupon available in the same year. In the absence of such record, the Committee is unable to satisfy itself that the borrowing was raised at the least cost consistent with requirement, and the excess cost must be regarded as avoidable within the meaning of ToR 4. The Committee illustrates the order of magnitude. Had the Rs 2,500 crore raised through Domestic Bond Series 3 and 4 in 2024-25 been raised instead on terms comparable to the NABARD NIDA drawings of the same year, the annual interest saving would have been of the order of Rs 55 crore, and over the ten-year tenure of those bonds, substantially more. The Committee does not suggest that the entire sum could in fact have been raised from NABARD; it offers the figure to show what is at stake in the absence of a documented comparison. 

##### **3.18.3 Negative interest carry on unutilised borrowed funds** 

The information made available by the statutory auditor of KIIFB to the Committee shows that on several occasions borrowed funds remained unutilised for months, during which they were parked in deposits earning between 6.5 and 7.5 per cent while carrying a funding cost of between 8.5 and 9.7 per cent. The resulting negative spread of roughly 150 to 250 basis points is a direct and quantifiable loss. Illustratively, a pool of Rs 10,000 crore held for six months at a negative spread of 200 basis points costs Rs 100 crore. 

In its note to the Committee, KIIFB explained that it was intended to build and maintain a minimum fund pool of Rs 10,000 crore before large projects were rolled out, and that a negative interest spread was therefore unavoidable. The Committee does not accept that this makes the loss unavoidable, for the reasons set out at paragraph 3.19.2. It records here only the consequence for cost: the negative carry is a recurring charge. 

##### **3.18.4 The escrow mechanism, the State’s ways and means position, and overdraft cost** 

There is a further cost, borne by the State rather than by KIIFB, which does not appear in any of the tables above. The Motor Vehicle Tax and Petroleum Cess earmarked under Section 7 do not reach KIIFB’s lenders as a single annual budgetary transfer. They are routed through an escrow arrangement under which sums are made available on a continuing, effectively daily, basis to service KIIFB’s debt. The State therefore parts with cash from its account with the Reserve Bank of India ahead of the point at which the corresponding expenditure would otherwise have fallen due, and must then replenish its balance. 

Two consequences follow for the State's Ways and Means position. First, the daily cash outflow depresses the State's minimum balance with the Reserve Bank of India and increases the frequency and duration of recourse to Special Drawing Facility (SDF) and Ways and Means (W&M) Advances and, beyond those, to Overdraft (OD). Second, overdraft from the Reserve Bank of India is charged at a penal rate above the repo rate which could be repo rate +2% for overdraft up to 100% of W&M 

35 

limit and repo rate + 5% for beyond the W&M limit. Ways and Means Advances are charged at the repo rate for the first 90 days and at repo rate +1% beyond 90 days. Special Drawing Facility (SDF) is charged at even below repo rate. Every rupee of additional recourse caused by the escrow outflow therefore carries a cost that is additional to, and entirely separate from, the coupon KIIFB pays on the underlying borrowing. The State is, in effect, paying twice: once through the interest embedded in KIIFB's borrowing, and again through the carrying cost of the liquidity it surrenders to service that borrowing. 

##### **3.19 Alignment of borrowing with project spending, and the size of the fund pool** 

##### **3.19.1 Alignment of borrowing with spending** 

ToR 3 requires the Committee to assess whether KIIFB’s borrowings were aligned with the objective of financing financially sustainable projects. Two kinds of alignment are involved: alignment of purpose, which is dealt with at paragraph 3.20, and alignment of timing, which is dealt with here. A mismatch between the timing of borrowing and the timing of project expenditure produces either delayed implementation or unnecessary interest cost, and usually both. 

The Committee observed that: 

- Borrowed funds remained unutilised for months on several occasions, parked in deposits at 6.5 to 7.5 per cent against a funding cost of 8.5 to 9.7 per cent. 

- KIIFB held Rs 6366.24 crore of sanctioned but undrawn facilities on 30 June 2026. This establishes that KIIFB is both able and willing to keep facilities in place without drawing them - which makes the parallel practice of drawing funds and parking them harder to explain, not easier. 

##### **3.19.2 Opportunity cost of KIIFB Reserves** 

As on June 30, 2026 KIIFB maintains a total fund of Rs. 9991.79 crore in its various accounts. Of this Rs. 4111.29 crore, in Treasury Deposits, is the Corpus Fund and Rs. 3007.33 crore is in the DSRA. The Corpus Fund is provided by the Government as grant, while the DSRA is funded through Section 7 transfers, which is replenished whenever required from the same source. Neither represents funds raised through KIIFB’s borrowings. Under Section 3(5) of the KIIF Act, KIIFB cannot place its borrowed funds in the State Treasury. Details furnished by KIIFB show that yield of treasury deposit is zero and the reserves in DSRA provide a yield of 6.67 percent in a year. 

These balances therefore do not constitute a borrowing cost or negative carry on KIIFB’s borrowings. However, they represent an opportunity cost arising from the institutional framework of KIIFB. Instead of KIIFB, if the Government had undertaken the same infrastructure expenditure directly through its budget, a separate Corpus Fund and DSRA requirements associated with KIIFB would not have arisen. Thus, the associated opportunity cost is a consequence of financing of 

36 

infrastructure investment through KIIFB rather than directly by the Government. The Government is not incurring any financing cost for the corpus fund as it is kept in the Treasury and its yield is zero; it can use this money without any costs for its liquidity management. However, reserves in DSRA account involve a financing cost for the Government of Keralam as it is created using government transfers and kept outside the treasury with a yield less than the borrowing cost of the State. The Committee calculated this cost and it comes to Rs. 23 crore in a year.<sup>3</sup> This cost must be viewed as an additional costs of the decision to undertake infrastructure financing through a separate statutory financing institution, rather than as a cost arising from KIIFB’s borrowing by itself. 

The Committee makes the following observation. The amount held under lien to lenders is duplicative credit enhancement. Every KIIFB borrowing already carries an unconditional guarantee of principal and interest which Section 9 of the Act obliges the Government to give, and for which KIIFB pays a guarantee commission to the Government. In addition, over Rs 3,000 crore of cash under lien is to pay twice for the same protection. Further, this reserves also costs around Rs. 23 crore for the government in a year. The Committee was shown no record of KIIFB having sought a waiver or reduction of the debt service reserve requirement on the strength of the statutory guarantee, or of any lender having been asked to price the guarantee into the reserve requirement.

If the premise holds, the State’s guarantee under Section 9 is contingent and the arrangement is fiscally neutral. If it fails, the guarantee is nominal and the State is simply borrowing at one remove and at a higher price. Table 3.15 tests the premise.

##### **3.21 Masala Bonds and Entry 37 of List I** 

The Comptroller and Auditor General, in the State Finances Audit Report for the year ended 31 March 2019, took the view that the Masala Bond issue encroached on the exclusive legislative field of the Union. The passage (page 42) reads: 

Further, from Table 1.34 above, it is observed that the maximum amount of borrowings is done from Masala bonds, which are, by their very nature, external commercial borrowings. However, entry 37 of List I of the 7th Schedule of Constitution of India gives the powers of raising foreign loans only to the Central Government. The entire repayment is being done through Government’s own revenue resources and therefore, such foreign borrowings are, for all practical purposes, borrowings of the State Government. Hence, they appear to be in violation of the provisions of the Indian Constitution and encroachment by the State on the powers of the Centre. The approval given by RBI to KIIFB to issue Masala bonds is also questionable because its permission has allowed the State Government to route its own foreign borrowings through KIIFB. If this model were to be followed by other States as well, then the external liabilities of the country would increase substantially, without the Central Government even knowing that such liabilities are being created. The purpose and intent of Entry 37 in List I would be defeated. 

38 

The contrary view, which the Committee records for completeness, rests on three propositions: that KIIFB is a body corporate with a legal personality distinct from the State and was the borrower in its own name; that the bonds were denominated in rupees, so that the State assumed no exchange risk and the transaction is not a “foreign loan” in the sense of a borrowing repayable in foreign currency; and that the issue was made under the External Commercial Borrowing framework with the specific approval of the Reserve Bank of India, the regulator designated by the Union for that purpose, for an amount of Rs 2,672.80 crore. 

The Committee does not consider it necessary or appropriate to return a final finding on the constitutional question. It is a question of some difficulty, it has not been adjudicated, and the circumstances that gave rise to it no longer subsist: since the bonds were fully redeemed on 27 March 2024, no liability under them remains outstanding, and no further issue has been made. The Committee accordingly leaves the constitutional question where the Comptroller and Auditor General left it, noting that the borrowing has been repaid in full. 

It does, however, record two points of continuing relevance. First, the structural objection the Comptroller and Auditor General identifies - that a route which allows States to create external liabilities outside the Union’s knowledge would defeat the purpose of Entry 37 - is not answered by the fact that this particular issue was repaid. Second, the Committee sees no case for a further issue of this kind, both because of the unresolved constitutional question and because, as shown at paragraph 3.18.1, the instrument was one of the most expensive KIIFB has used and the claimed benchmark benefit is not established on the record. 

#### **PART IV - OBSERVATIONS AND COMMENTS** 

The Committee’s observations follow from the analysis in Parts I to III and are stated here in summary form. The supporting reasoning is not repeated. 

##### **On resource mobilisation from the State (ToR 3, ToR 5)** 

1. The State transferred Rs 24,764.64 crore to KIIFB under Section 7 up to 30 June 2026. Over 2021-22 to 2024-25, transfers of Rs 11,869.28 crore met 99.4 per cent of KIIFB’s debt service of Rs 11,944.18 crore. In net terms the State’s transfers finance debt service, not infrastructure. 

2. The 2016 amendment converted the Government’s discretionary power to guarantee under Section 9 into an obligation, and removed the statutory ceiling that the 1999 and 2002 Acts had imposed. Every KIIFB borrowing since 2016 has been guaranteed as of statutory course rather than on a case-by-case assessment. 

##### **On borrowing and the requirement of Government sanction (ToR 3, ToR 5, ToR 7)** 

3. KIIFB had borrowed Rs 42,193.45 crore of Rs 48559.69 crore sanctioned, repaid Rs 10512.30 crore, and carried Rs 31,681.15 crore outstanding as on 30 June 2026. 

4. No evidence was placed before the Committee to establish that the previous sanction required by Section 8(1) was accorded in each case by the Finance Department of the 39 

Government of Keralam, as distinct from the Board, its Executive Committee, or KIIFB’s own finance wing acting under delegation pursuant to Section 8A. Where the proposing authority is also the sanctioning authority, the statutory safeguard becomes selfcertification. This does not uphold the spirit of Section 8, whatever the seniority of the persons involved, and the Fund Trustee and Advisory Commission under Section 6D does not fill the gap, its remit being fidelity of application rather than appraisal of borrowing terms. 

5. The substitution of Section 8(2) of 1999 Act by Section 8(4) of 2016 Act shifted the initiative in issuing and redeeming the Board’s instruments from the Government to the Board. 

##### **On cost of borrowing (ToR 4)** 

6. KIIFB’s cost of borrowing exceeded the State’s own open market borrowing cost in every year of comparison, by between 85 and 179 basis points, with the sole exception of the NABARD NIDA drawings of 2024-25. On the present outstanding portfolio, a spread of 125 basis points represents an avoidable charge of roughly Rs 396 crore a year. 

7. Instruments of materially different cost were used within the same year - a range of 216 basis points in 2024-25, including borrowings at 7.25 per cent and a bond issue at 9.42 per cent ten weeks apart. No evidence was furnished, for any year, to explain why the more expensive instrument was preferred over the cheaper one then available. 

8. Borrowed funds remained unutilised for months and were parked at 6.5 to 7.5 per cent against a funding cost of 8.5 to 9.7 per cent. 

9. A further cost falls on the State and is nowhere recorded: the escrow mechanism surrenders cash on a continuing basis, depressing the State’s balance with the Reserve Bank of India and increasing recourse to Ways and Means Advances and Overdraft, the latter at a penal rate. The Finance Department should quantify this and bring it into the reckoning of the true cost of the arrangement. 

10. The Masala Bond, at 9.723 per cent per annum against the State’s 8.31 per cent, cost approximately Rs 152 crore more in interest over its life than the same sum raised by the State would have cost, before placement and listing expenses. With placement and list cost of Rs. 29.73 crore, the excess cost of Masala bond is Rs. 181.73 crore.  KIIFB’s claim that the issue lowered its subsequent domestic borrowing cost is not established: the spread over the State’s cost did not narrow after 2019, and no contemporaneous evaluation of the claimed benefit was produced. 

##### **On alignment of borrowing with spending and the fund pool (ToR 3, ToR 4, ToR 7)** 

11. The amount held under lien to lenders is duplicative credit enhancement. Every KIIFB borrowing already carries an unconditional guarantee of principal and interest which Section 9 of the Act obliges the Government to give, and for which KIIFB pays a guarantee commission to the Government. In addition, over Rs 3,000 crore of cash under lien is to 

40 

pay twice for the same protection and this has an opportunity cost of Rs. 23 crore to the Government in a year. The Committee was shown no record of KIIFB having sought a waiver or reduction of the debt service reserve requirement on the strength of the statutory guarantee, or of any lender having been asked to price the guarantee into the reserve requirement. 

##### **On constitutional and legal compliance (ToR 5, ToR 9)** 

12. The Comptroller and Auditor General has held that KIIFB’s borrowings are off-budget borrowings bypassing Article 293, lacking legislative approval, and that a guarantee is contingent only where the borrowing entity repays from its own resources. Applying that test to the figures, KIIFB’s liability is not contingent: project revenues met 20.5 per cent of interest and 9.5 per cent of debt service over 2021-22 to 2023-24. The Committee concurs. 

13. The Fifteenth Finance Commission recommended that all tiers of government resist further additions to off-budget transactions. The Sixteenth Finance Commission observed at paragraph 12.22 that escrowing government revenues instead of crediting them to the Consolidated Fund “could also be unconstitutional under Article 266” and recommended that States discontinue the practice entirely. The Government of India has accepted the borrowing-ceiling recommendation in principle and has stated that the off-budget borrowing recommendations will be examined separately. 

14. Under the Net Borrowing Ceiling guidelines of 31 March 2022, borrowings serviced out of State budgets or by assignment of taxes or cess are treated as the State’s own borrowings for Article 293(3) consent. Rs 25,932.55 crore has been deducted from Keralam’s borrowing space on this account. The original rationale for KIIFB - raising capital without consuming borrowing space - no longer exists, while the cost premium over State borrowing remains. 

15. On the Masala Bond and Entry 37 of List I, the Committee does not return a final finding. The bonds were redeemed in full on 27 March 2024 with no liability outstanding. The Committee records the Comptroller and Auditor General’s view, notes the contrary arguments, and recommends against any further issue of this kind. 

16. The Committee observed that a systematic methodology that could have ensured adherence to the Kerala Fiscal Responsibility Act, 2003, was absent. KIIFB did not adopt a consistent accounting system that could have aligned with the government, to avoid discrepancies.  No system of any medium-term framework was adhered to, as required under principles of financial prudence. Thus, institutional predictability vis-a-vis government liabilities did not follow any efficient pattern. The absence of alignment with periodical break-ups, like quarterly, half-yearly etc., blurred analytical integrity. 

41 

##### **On the revenue-generating premise and mobilisation from non-residents (ToR 3, ToR 5, ToR 7)** 

17. Only 19.72 per cent of borrowing over 2021-22 to 2023-24 was lent for revenuegenerating projects, and the proportion fell year on year from 34.2 to 7.4 per cent. Section 7 was drafted as a residual provision to meet a shortfall; it has become the primary source of repayment. The mismatch is structural, originating in the 2016 extension of the Act to social-sector projects that can never earn revenue for the Fund. 

18. No evidence was placed before the Committee that a programme of revenue-generating projects was identified and taken up, on any material scale, during the years in which the State was funding KIIFB under Section 7, nor of any review or course correction addressing the widening gap. The Committee regards this as a systemic deficiency. 

19. The Pravasi Dividend Scheme embeds an undisclosed State subsidy: KIIFB pays 8.30 to 9.00 per cent, the subscriber is assured 10 per cent, and the State bridges the difference and underwrites the Welfare Board’s liability. The effective cost to the State is 10 per cent. 

20. Chit fund based mobilisation of Rs 2241.85 crore through KSFE, the Committee does not find the arrangement unlawful, but finds that its legality rests on a single narrow gateway. Section 14 of the Chit Funds Act, 1982 permits chit moneys to be used only for chit business, loans to non-prized subscribers, deposits with approved banks, or investment in trustee securities within the meaning of Section 20 of the Indian Trusts Act, 1882. KIIFB’s instruments can qualify only under the last of these. Section 20 of the Indian Trusts Act was substituted in its entirety by Act 34 of 2016, removing the enumerated categories (which had included State-guaranteed securities) and making eligibility depend on the trust instrument or a Central Government notification - two years before the placements began in November 2018. No contemporaneous legal opinion, no permission of the Registrar of Chits, and no record of any examination of these provisions was placed before the Committee by KIIFB or KSFE. The Secretary (Law) has since advised that the investment is within Section 20 of the Indian Trusts Act; the Committee takes note of the advice.

##### **4.2.2 Share of revenue-generating projects** 

KIIFB classifies a subset of its projects as revenue-generating. These are expected to service and repay their loans from the project's own assigned revenue stream, rather than from Section 7 transfers. The Appendix to this chapter lists the revenue-generating projects financed by KIIFB till September 15, 2026. The list shows these projects are mainly in Industrial Infrastructure, Information Technology, Power, Tourism, Transport and Commercial Infrastructure. 

Data collected from KIIFB shows that as on September 15, 2026, KIIFB had sanctioned 44 revenue-generating projects worth Rs. 22,701.44 crore, disbursing Rs. 8,881.80 crore and receiving Rs. 4,198.32 crore in repayments from these projects. These figures show that revenue-generating projects account for 22.63 per cent of total approved project amounts and 20.98 per cent of total funds released - that is, only about one-fifth of KIIFB's project funding is classified as revenue-generating, with the remainder's repayment falling on budgetary transfers. 

A closer look at the Appendix’s list of revenue-generating projects reveals that out of 44 projects, only 6 have made any repayment to KIIFB. However, this repayment needs a careful 

64 

scrutiny, as it is also possible that SPVs may have made the repayment using the budgetary transfers, instead of the revenue generated by the project. Only if the repayment was made out of the revenue generated by the project, should we consider the project as “revenue-generating” in its true sense. Otherwise, it is just a budget financed project where initial funding came from KIIFB and repayment to KIIFB was made using budgetary transfers. Therefore, the Committee examined the source of repayment of these six projects and found that repayments from three projects, namely (a) Transgrid 2.0, (b) First Building at Technocity, and (c) Petrochemical Park, Kochi came from the revenue generated by the projects, and hence they can be classified as revenue generating in a meaningful sense. These projects together made a repayment of Rs. 3351.90 crore. The repayments from the remaining three projects were made using the transfers from the government to the concerned SPV. 

The majority of the revenue-generating projects are not, in fact, revenue-generating; moreover, by its own admission, KIIFB agrees that most of the funds it releases for other projects are not backed by a project-specific revenue stream and depend on future transfers under Section 7 of the KIIF Act for debt servicing. The fiscal implications of this reliance are discussed in the context of KIIFB's operating expenses in Part Three of this Chapter. 

##### **4.2.3 Departmental view on selection and implementation of projects** 

The Committee collected views of departments on selection and implementation of projects by KIIFB. Departments acknowledged certain operational advantages of the KIIFB framework. It provided access to substantial funding outside the constraints of the annual State budget, which was particularly useful for capital-intensive sectors such as roads and hospitals. It also enabled departments to undertake larger project packages rather than implementing works in smaller phases, provided some flexibility in project selection, and, in several cases, facilitated faster sanctioning than the conventional Plan and Finance Department procedures. The use of Special Purpose Vehicles (SPVs) also provided an alternative mechanism for project execution and helped overcome some of the limitations of conventional departmental implementation. 

However, these operational advantages were accompanied by significant institutional concerns. Once projects entered the KIIFB framework, departments had limited control over the timing of fund availability, as disbursement was governed by KIIFB's procedures and cash flows rather than the departmental budget cycle. KIIFB's separate technical and financial appraisal procedures also reduced the scope for departmental intervention and, in some cases, contributed to cost escalation. Funds were tied to sanctioned projects, limiting the flexibility to 

65 

reallocate resources in response to changing priorities. There was also a significant decline in transparency in project selection. In the initial years, projects were selected primarily on the basis of the technical assessment of departments and their ranking of sectoral needs. In later years, however, project selection became less transparent and increasingly detached from departmental and sectoral priorities. Departments were consequently required, in several instances, to implement projects selected on grounds other than their assessed needs or sectoral priorities, while the basis on which KIIFB accorded priority to such projects was not transparent. Heavy reliance on KIIFB also reduced the incentive and need for departments to strengthen their own capabilities in project preparation, financial management and implementation, thereby creating institutional dependence on KIIFB. 

The Committee considers that these longer-term institutional implications are more consequential than the immediate operational advantages of the KIIFB model. KIIFB operated largely outside the State's formal planning process, weakening the linkage between infrastructure investment and integrated sectoral planning and creating scope for project-byproject rather than system-wide decisions. The emphasis on individual projects also meant that wider requirements, such as integrated urban transport, multimodal connectivity and long-term project viability, did not always receive adequate attention. The State's institutional capabilities in alternative modes of infrastructure financing, particularly PPPs, were also not developed commensurately. Similarly, while SPVs were widely used for execution, there was no systematic effort to build durable capabilities within them or within the concerned departments. Thus, while KIIFB expanded access to capital and facilitated the implementation of infrastructure projects, its mode of operation also had implications for financial control, transparency in project prioritisation, departmental capabilities, planning integration and institutional capacity. These longer-term effects substantially qualify the significance of its immediate operational advantages. 

##### **4.2.4 Statutory and policy compliance** 

Following Article 46 of the Indian Constitution, Keralam's Five-Year Plans adopt measures to protect and promote the interests of Scheduled Castes (SCs) and Scheduled Tribes (STs), including Special Component Plans for these communities - respectively, the Scheduled Caste Sub Plan (SCSP) and the Tribal Sub Plan (TSP). These sub-plans require that resources be earmarked from the State plan outlay in proportion to the share of SCs and STs in the State population; accordingly, the prescribed shares of total plan outlay are 9.81 per cent for the 

66 

SCSP and 2.83 per cent for the TSP, together at least 12.64 per cent of total plan outlay. However, the Committee could find no statutory or policy provision requiring KIIFB-financed infrastructure investment to be allocated in proportions similar to those prescribed under the SCSP/TSP. 

This paragraph, therefore, examines KIIFB’s infrastructure spending on projects exclusively benefiting SC and ST communities, regardless of whether such spending is mandated by any statutory or policy provision. Table 4.4 shows that, as on June 30, 2026, KIIFB had approved ten projects for SC/ST development with an outlay of Rs. 182 crore - around 0.18 per cent of the total approved amount - of which only Rs. 87 crore had been released, which comes to around 0.21 per cent of the total released amount. A note from KIIFB on the nature of these projects shows that it has supported a number of infrastructure projects proposed by the SC/ST Development Department, specifically intended to strengthen educational, residential and vocational infrastructure for SC/ST students across Keralam. These include Industrial Training Institutes (ITIs), Model Residential Schools (MRS), Pre-Matric and Post-Matric Hostels, vocational training facilities, pre-examination training facilities and related infrastructure. Placed against the statutory benchmark of 12.64 per cent of Plan outlay prescribed for the SCSP and TSP together, the finding that only 0.18 per cent of KIIFB’s approved outlay and 0.21 per cent of the amount released was allocated to projects exclusively for SC/ST development is a matter of concern. Had KIIFB’s infrastructure spending been subject to a binding allocation requirement comparable to that applicable under the SCSP/TSP, SC/ST development would have received a substantially larger share of KIIFB’s resources than it actually received.  This points to a gap in the statutory/policy architecture, particularly as KIIFB has increasingly become an important channel for financing the State’s infrastructure investment outside the regular Plan process. Therefore, the Committee is of the view that, as KIIFB’s role in infrastructure financing has expanded, the absence of an explicit and enforceable equity safeguard comparable to the SCSP/TSP mechanism constitutes a policy gap that warrants attention by the Finance Department and the State Planning Board. 

##### **4.2.5 Oversight by Department of Finance and Kerala State Planning Board** 

As noted in Chapter II, KIIFB's Board includes the Finance Minister as Vice-Chairperson, with Secretary (Finance) and Secretary (Finance-Resources) as members; similarly, the Executive Committee has the Finance Minister as Chairperson, with the same two Secretaries as members. This constitutes the first level of Finance Department oversight over KIIFB. However, KIIFB's day-to-day functioning carries financial implications that call for closer 

67 

oversight, and there have been instances where KIIFB's decisions departed from Finance Department norms. For instance, G.O.(Rt) No.5457/2025/Fin dated 27.06.2025, which revised price-escalation guidelines for KIIFB-funded projects, was issued without being vetted by the Finance Department. Following this and similar instances, the Finance Department has progressively centralised control over KIIFB's administrative files - first requiring that Government Orders issued by the Finance (Infrastructure) Department be vetted by the Finance Department, and later transferring all KIIFB-related files to the Finance (PU-B) Wing. 

On the State Planning Board's oversight of KIIFB's activities, the Committee's assessment is that this was limited to the Vice-Chairman of the State Planning Board serving as a member of the KIIFB Board.

##### **Part IV: Observations and Comments** 

Based on the examination in the preceding parts of this Chapter, the Committee makes the following observations and comments on Items 6, 7 and 8 of its Terms of Reference. 

##### **On the equity and priority-orientation of fund allocation (Item 6)** 

1. Allocation of infrastructure outlay is heavily concentrated in a few districts, while several districts, including some of the State's more backward and hilly regions, receive a disproportionately small share; the pattern is even more pronounced in the land acquisition pool. 

2. Sectoral allocation is similarly concentrated among a few large-spending departments, principally the Public Works Department, leaving the remaining departments to share a comparatively small residual pool. 

3. The Committee could not identify a documented framework by which KIIFB or the Finance Department weighs developmental priorities or inter-district equity when approving projects; a project's entry into the KIIFB pipeline appears to depend substantially on the readiness of the sponsoring department and SPV rather than on an explicit equity criterion. 

4. The share of projects focusing exclusively on SC/ST development in total allocation to projects is extremely low compared to SCSP and TSP allocation in State Plan. 

5. Given the large expansion in the infrastructure spending by KIIFB, the absence of an explicit and enforceable equity safeguard comparable to the SCSP/TSP mechanism constitutes a policy gap that warrants attention by the Finance Department and the State Planning Board. 

6. The Committee was not furnished with district-wise data on developmental indicators, such as backwardness indices or population shares, that would allow a more rigorous test of equity than the simple equal-share benchmark used in this Chapter. Such data, if maintained by the State Planning Board, should be used in future to assess KIIFB's allocations. 

70 

##### **On expenditures and practices warranting detailed examination (Item 7)** 

7. Publicity and promotional expenditures are the clearest instance identified during the Committee's examination. KIIFB's own Audit Committee and Independent Directors have flagged SOP deviations, vendor-selection irregularities and documentation deficiencies in relation to Rs. 203.72 crore of such expenditure in 2025-26 alone; the Committee endorses the comprehensive audit already directed by the Audit Committee. 

8. The apparent mismatch between this figure and the amount recorded against Advertisement & Promotion in KIIFB's own operating-expense accounts should be reconciled, as it bears on the completeness and classification of KIIFB's financial reporting more generally. 

9. Finance Cost is now, by a wide margin, the dominant component of KIIFB's operating expenditure. The true measure of KIIFB's financial position therefore lies less in its administrative costs than in its debt-servicing burden, and the Committee recommends that this cost be tracked as a share of releases and of the State's own fiscal indicators. 

10. The issuance of G.O.(Rt) No.5457/2025/Fin without prior vetting by the Finance Department is an instance of KIIFB issuing an order with financial implications outside the normal vetting channel. The Committee considers this warrants examination beyond the specific case, to establish whether it was an isolated lapse or a more systemic practice. 

11. The very large, concentrated commitments in the land acquisition pool, particularly in Kannur district, and the wide gaps between amounts approved and released across the revenue-generating projects listed in the Appendix are both areas the Committee recommends for more detailed audit or financial examination. 

12. Six projects, out of 44 projects, shown to have made repayments to KIIFB. However, a closer look at the repayments received from these projects revealed that only three made repayments from the revenue generated by the projects and the remaining three repayments were made using the budgetary transfers received by the respective SPVs from the Government.  This suggests that the revenue generation classification of KIIFB’s projects is fiscally misleading. The correct data needs to be compiled and presented transparently. 

##### **On the oversight role of the Finance Department and the State Planning Board (Item 8)** 

13. The Finance Department's oversight of KIIFB is built into the institutional design, through its representation on the Board and the Executive Committee, but the evidence before the Committee suggests that this oversight hardly operated and even where it operated, it did so in a largely ex-post, reactive manner. 

71 

14. As far as the Committee could ascertain, the State Planning Board's oversight of KIIFB was limited to the ex-officio membership of its Vice-Chairman on the KIIFB Board. The Committee found no evidence of the State Planning Board exercising a substantive appraisal or screening role over project selection, including on consistency with Five-Year Plan priorities or SCSP/TSP-type equity considerations.

### **Part I: Departures from Constitutional Provisions and Rules of Business** 

In Chapter II, a mention was made of the changes and deviations that occurred in the objectives, mandate and role originally envisaged for KIIFB in 2014. This Part lists the instances in which the Constitution, the Rules of Business and fiscal laws were not followed or were put under strain. Their fiscal and governance consequences are discussed in Part II, and the recommendations in Part III take both into account. 

Major changes were effected by the Kerala Infrastructure Investment Fund (Amendment) Act, 2016 (Act 15 of 2016), and by the Government Orders and the Scheme issued under it. Over the next few years many more deviations occurred. These aggravated the “fundamental flaws” that, in the words of Kerala’s Fiscal Health: A Status Report (the White Paper, page 147), existed “ab initio” in the constitution of KIIFB. They adversely affected both the governance and the fiscal health of Keralam, without delivering on the original promise. 

##### **6.1 Constitutional and Fiscal-Law Concerns** 

1. Article 266(1) of the Constitution provides: 

   - …all revenues received by the Government of a State, all loans raised by that Government by the issue of treasury bills, loans or ways and means advances and all moneys received by that Government in repayment of loans shall form one Consolidated Fund to be entitled “the Consolidated Fund of the State”. 

Article 266(3) provides that no moneys out of the Consolidated Fund shall be appropriated except in accordance with law and in the manner provided in the Constitution, and Articles 202 to 204 require expenditure to be authorised by the Legislature (para 3.14). 

Under G.O.(P) No. 158/2018/Fin dated 05.10.2018, the Government decided to escrow, directly to KIIFB, a share of Motor Vehicles Tax rising to 50 per cent from the fifth year, and the entire Cess on Petroleum products. Section 7 as amended in 2016 does require the Government to make provision in the Annual Budget, and the transfers are budgeted under voted heads (para 3.2). But the escrow, as per the Government Order cited above, makes these revenues available to KIIFB directly, and the accounting treatment adopted raises concerns of its own (para 3.1). The Sixteenth Finance Commission has observed that escrowing government revenues instead of crediting them to the Consolidated Fund “could also be unconstitutional under Article 266” (16<sup>th</sup> Finance Commission Report, para 12.22). 

83 

In the view of the Committee, the Government order cited above prescribing the routing of these revenue streams directly to KIIFB is at odds with the scheme of Articles 266, 202 and 204 and raises a serious Constitutional question. The Sixteenth Finance Commission has put the point as a possibility, and the matter has not been adjudicated. The Committee also noted that the GO cited above is at variance with the original provision relating to escrow in the KIIFB (Amendment) Act 2016. The Committee therefore does not return a final finding on constitutionality. 

2. Article 293(1) of the Constitution of India empowers a State to borrow within India on the security of its Consolidated Fund, within limits fixed by the State Legislature. The CAG reported (State Finances Audit Report for 2018-19) that KIIFB’s borrowings are off-budget borrowings that bypass the limits on Government borrowing under Article 293, are not shown in the budget documents or the accounts, and therefore do not have legislative approval (para 3.15). 

3. Article 293(3) of the Constitution of India requires a State Government to obtain the consent of the Union Government before raising any loan if the State already has outstanding liabilities to the Union Government. Since KIIFB’s debt is serviced from assigned State tax revenues (Motor Vehicles Tax and Petroleum Cess), the Government of India, acting on the Fifteenth Finance Commission’s recommendation, now treats such borrowings as the State’s own for the purpose of consent under Article 293(3) (Net Borrowing Ceiling guidelines of 31 March 2022, para 3.17). The CAG also made the same point (para 3.15). 

4. Entry 37 of the Union List (Seventh Schedule), Constitution of India, places “foreign loans” exclusively within the legislative competence of the Union. The CAG observed that the Masala Bond issue, being in substance an external commercial borrowing repaid from State revenues, appeared to violate this entry. The Committee has not returned a final finding, since the bonds were rupee-denominated, issued with the approval of the Reserve Bank of India and fully redeemed on 27 March 2024 (para 3.21). 

5. Kerala Fiscal Responsibility Act 2003  Sections 4(2)(b) and 4(2)(c) prescribe limits on off-budget liabilities, transparent fiscal deficits, and provide a total debt-to-GSDP ceiling and a  3 percent fiscal deficit cap. Section 5 (2)(b) under Measures for Fiscal Transparency (Off-Budget Disclosure Mandates) requires the State Government to disclose in the budget all outstanding contractual liabilities, committed liabilities in major works, off-budget borrowings, and contingent liabilities created by way of 

84 

guarantees. CAG repeatedly noted that the off-budget shifting of infrastructure expenditure through KIIFB, resulted in the State underreporting its actual fiscal deficit and in exceeding the prescribed aggregate debt ceilings.

**Part II: Fiscal Impact and Governance Consequences of KIIFB: A Broad Overview** 

Chapters III and IV examined in detail the fund mobilisation and fund disbursal of KIIFB and the Committee identified several important issues with significant fiscal consequences. Some of them are listed below: 

1. The Act was designed around revenue-earning projects. Only about 20.98 per cent of the funds released by KIIFB are for projects classified as “revenue-generating”, and even this classification is apocryphal, since only three of the 44 such projects have so far been shown to repay from their own revenues (Chapter IV, para 4.2.2). 

2. Section 7 of the KIIFB (Amendment) Act 2016 was meant to be a residual provision. It became the primary source of repayment: transfers from the State met 99.4 per cent of KIIFB’s debt service over 2021-22 to 2024-25 (Chapter III, Table 3.2). 

3. The Government’s power to guarantee under Section 9 of the Act became a duty (Chapter III, para 3.1). 

4. Section 8(4), inserted in 2016, moved the initiative in issuing and redeeming the Board’s instruments from the Government (“as the Government may direct” in the 1999 Act) to the Board (“as decided by the Board subject to the directions of the Government”). The requirement of previous Government sanction for borrowing under Section 8(1) was retained, but was observed in form and, so far as the record discloses, not in substance. The Committee found no clear evidence of the Finance Department’s sanction for individual borrowings. The shift in Section 8(4) is consistent with that pattern, but does not by itself explain it, since Section 8(1) continued to require previous sanction. (Chapter III paras 3.5 and 3.12). 

5. The scope of the Act was widened to the social sector and to land acquisition, neither of which generates revenue for the Fund in the way the original design assumed (Chapter III, para 3.20, and Chapter IV, para 4.1). 

6. Under the Government of India’s Net Borrowing Ceiling guidelines of 31 March 2022, which apply from 2020-21, KIIFB’s borrowing is counted as the State’s own borrowing. Rs 25,932.55 crore has been deducted from Keralam’s borrowing space on this account (Chapter III, Table 3.11). The original rationale of raising capital without using that space no longer holds. 

7. KIIFB’s cost of borrowing exceeded that of State’s open market borrowing in every year of comparison, by 85 to 179 basis points since 2018-19 (Table 3.12). Applied to the outstanding debt of Rs 31,681.15 crore (as on June 30, 2026; see Table 3.3), that

This higher cost buys no extra borrowing space, since the borrowing is counted against the State’s ceiling in any event.

9. The above comparison can also be interpreted differently. Had the State undertaken infrastructure investment directly instead of routing it through KIIFB, it could have spent Rs. 61,678.46 crore, compared with the Rs. 48,574.82 crore disbursed or held as cash by KIIFB. Further, the prioritisation of such spending would have been subject to the deliberative process involving the Legislature and the Kerala State Planning Board, and the plan allocations for the Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes would have applied. Thus, the diversion of infrastructure spending through KIIFB entails not only a substantial financial loss but also a significant welfare loss.

14. In addition to the high fiscal and welfare costs of KIIFB noted above, the Committee also finds that the working of KIIFB over the last ten years has weakened the system of governance. During the debate on the Bill in 2016 in the State Legislature, several members expressed their concern about the structure of the proposed Board, stating that it would create a 'Super Cabinet’. One of the members stated that the doubt of the people was whether it would be an ‘extra-Constitutional’ entity. Some members felt that after a department gives administrative sanction, the programme would come before the (Executive) Committee. Then the final decision would be taken by the Board. So, the Department's powers ended up being transferred to KIIFB. Members also pointed out the manner in which KIIFB could erode the control of the departments through its project evaluation team. The expansion of the definition of infrastructure to include 23 items - mirroring the remit of Government departments in the Rules of Business - created a vastly expanded role for KIIFB. 

15. The feedback received from the departments indicates that, on the whole, KIIFB’s activities weakened the capabilities of Government departments. The departments also acknowledged some advantages, notably access to capital outside the annual budget, larger project packages and faster sanction. (Chapter IV, para 4.2.3).  However, as we found this access to capital did not benefit the State. The departments also pointed some negative consequences, particularly stressing the following two points.  (1) Heavy reliance on KIIFB pool for capital works eroded departments' own technical capabilities, and reduced their need and incentive to pursue funds through regular budgetary channels and other sources. (2)  While Government of India and many other states developed expertise in Public-Private Partnerships, Kerala's dependence on KIIFB slowed the State's own efforts toward PPPs. Just as it will take several years for the Finance Department to discharge the accumulated financial liabilities, it may take some years for the other departments to recover their capability to do full justice to their remit. 

16. The Committee concurs with the CAG that the liability for KIIFB’s borrowing is not a contingent liability. KIIFB’s own revenue from projects met only 20.5 per cent of its interest and 9.5 per cent of its debt service in 2021-22 to 2023-24, and State transfers met 99.4 per cent of its debt service. The liability is therefore a direct charge on State revenues, and the guarantee under Section 9 is, in substance, nominal and the liability is actual (Chapter III, para 3.15).

17. Equity in the use of funds was not built into the design. Approved infrastructure outlay is concentrated in three districts (Thiruvananthapuram, Kannur and Ernakulam, 41.6 per cent), and in the land acquisition pool Kannur alone accounts for 52.9 per cent. Taking both pools together, Kannur’s approved outlay is Rs 23,024.85 crore (22.9 per cent of the total of Rs 100,341 crore). The Public Works Department accounts for 52.6 per cent of approved infrastructure outlay, and projects exclusively for Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes for 0.18 per cent. No documented framework weighed developmental priorities or inter-district equity (Chapter IV, paras 4.2 and 4.2.4). 

18. The structure carries costs beyond interest. Borrowed funds were left unutilised for months while carrying a funding cost above the deposit yield (para 3.18.3); a debt service reserve of over Rs 3,000 crore is held under lien although every borrowing already carries the State’s guarantee (para 3.19.2); and the daily escrow of State revenues adds to the State’s recourse to Ways and Means Advances and overdraft (para 3.18.4). Mobilisation through the Pravasi Dividend Scheme carries an assured return of 10 per cent, and the legal basis for investing chit fund money in KIIFB’s bonds rests on a single statutory gateway (paras 3.9 and 3.13). 

19. Expenditure and establishment were not adequately controlled. Publicity and promotional expenditure of Rs 203.72 crore in 2025-26 is under audit examination, against Rs 21.16 crore booked under that head (Chapter IV, para 4.3). KIIFB has 498 persons against forty sanctioned posts.  Its subsidiary KIIFCON works almost wholly for KIIFB-funded and Government projects (Chapter V, para 5.3). The Finance Department’s and the State Planning Board’s oversight was limited and largely after the event (Chapter IV, para 4.2.5). 

The Committee is of the view that both financial and administrative recovery will need continued and consistent support of the Government. 

The Committee does not question the value of some infrastructure that has been created. However, on account of the deviations from its mandate, the manner in which this infrastructure was funded and governed placed excessive burden on the Government of Keralam. The recommendations that follow are meant to restructure KIIFB comprehensively.

### **Part III: Recommendations of the Committee** 

The Committee makes the following recommendations for restructuring KIIFB: 

Relevance and sustainability of KIIFB (Terms of Reference 9): The Committee finds no case for the continuation of KIIFB as a body that raises funds and finances projects. Its original rationale, raising capital without using the State’s borrowing space, has been extinguished, since its borrowing is now counted against that space (para 3.17). It borrows at a cost above the State’s own (Table 3.12). The premise of revenue-generating projects has not held (para 3.20), and its liabilities are in substance direct liabilities of the State (para 3.15). Its working has also departed from the Rules of Business (Part 1), and the Government of India’s policy on off-budget borrowing leaves no room for it as a separate borrower. Accordingly, the Committee makes the following recommendations. 

1. **Performance Audit of KIIFB** : CAG/AG Keralam may be requested conduct a Performance Audit of KIIFB under Section 14 (1) of the CAG’s DPC Act 1971, for a period of ten years commencing from 2016-17 to 2025-26, at the earliest possible. 

2. **Amendments to the Borrowing Provisions:** KIIFB shall henceforth not be permitted to raise funds through market borrowing. To give effect to this recommendation, Sections 8, 9 and other relevant provisions of the KIIFB (Amendment) Act, 2016, would be required to be omitted. 

3. **Discharging of current liabilities** : Government shall make adequate provision in the Annual Budget for meeting the repayment obligations arising from the bonds/debentures/loans of KIIFB, as set out in Table 6.1. The payment shall be made to the post-amendment structure of KIIFB to comply with its debt repayment obligations. 

4. **Existing Infrastructure Projects:** Infrastructure projects already initiated by KIIFB, subsequently prioritised by the Government, and at various stages of implementation may be completed by the respective departments with the support of implementing agencies. The funds required for completing these projects shall be provided as capital expenditure in the Annual Budget over the next five years, beginning in 2026-27, as set out in Table 6.3. 

5. **Debt Restructuring of KIIFB:** The Government shall take all necessary steps to replace/swap KIIFB’s existing high-cost debt with lower-cost liabilities, wherever feasible, to reduce its interest burden. 

95 

6. **Cancellation of Escrow order** : The order G.O.(P) No. 158/2018/Fin dated 05.10.2018, which provides for escrowing a share of revenue from Motor Vehicles Tax and Cess on Petroleum Products directly to the account of KIIFB, shall be cancelled once the Government of Keralam takes over the debt obligations of KIIFB. 

**7. Reallocation and re-deployment of staff** : As KIIFB will no longer mobilise market funds or implement infrastructure projects, the staff engaged in these activities may be rationalised as follows. (1) The staff on direct contract or contract through CMD may be maintained till the expiry of their contract. (2) Staff engaged in project appraisal, (including ESG group) and monitoring may be absorbed by the Kerala State Planning Board for the preparation and implementation of 15<sup>th</sup> Five-Year Plan projects. (3) Finance (Infrastructure) wing of KIIFB may be made part of the Finance Department, as was earlier, and staff on deputation from other departments may be repatriated to their parent departments. (4) The pay scales and other incentives of residual KIIFB staff shall be relooked and rationalised. (5) Since CEO of KIIFB can no longer make appointments, Section 10 of the KIIFB (Amendment) Act 2016 would be required to be omitted. 

8. **Relocation of KIFCON and KIFML:** KIFCON, the consulting subsidiary of KIIFB, may be brought under the Kerala State Planning Board for helping preparation of externally financed projects for local bodies. Similarly, The Kerala State Industrial Development Corporation (KSIDC) may take over KIIFB’s shareholding in Kerala Infrastructure Fund Management Limited (KIFML), presently an associated company of KIIFB.
